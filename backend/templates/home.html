A crop needs land, money, and someone to farm it.

Those three rarely sit with the same person. Bhoomi Share does two things about that: it lets an investor fund one season on one named parcel and take an agreed share of the harvest, and it lets a landowner who isn’t farming hand the land to a farmer who will — on paper, for a fixed term.

We farm 15 acres as a family. Both models run there first — our own capital, our own soil, a full season of numbers — before we take money or land from anyone else.

For investors and growers

Fund a season, share the harvest.

You are not buying land and you are not buying a share of a company. You are funding the inputs and labour of one crop cycle on one parcel, and taking an agreed slice of what that parcel sells for.

Pick a parcel with a season plan already on it — crop, sowing window, input budget, expected quintals, the split. Put in the capital side. The grower farms it and reports through the season.

For landowners and farmers

Land sitting idle is worth more with someone farming it.

Most of this already happens — by word of mouth, between families, on no paper at all. That works until it doesn’t. Four steps, and the third one is the whole point.

01

List or search

Acreage, survey number, water source and hours, soil, road access, and what was last grown on it. Farmers search by district and by what they can actually irrigate.

02

Agree on terms

Rent per acre per season, or a share of the crop. Who pays for borewell power, who repairs the bunds, who keeps the residue, what happens if the rain fails.

03

Sign a proper license

A fixed-term license drafted for your state, signed and witnessed. Not a handshake, and deliberately not an open-ended tenancy — see the fine print below for why that distinction matters.

04

Renew or move on

At the end of the term the land comes back to you, with a record of how it was farmed. Renew with the same farmer if it went well; list it again if it didn’t.

The fine print

We would rather grow slowly than fall foul of SEBI.

Two pieces of Indian law sit directly across this idea. Neither makes it impossible; both decide how it has to be built. We would rather put them on the front page than in a footnote — the long version is here.

Money

Small pools, and a lawyer’s name on the structure

Pooling money from the public and managing it for a share of the profits can qualify as a Collective Investment Scheme under the SEBI Act — and running one unregistered is not a risk we will take with someone else’s savings. So: capped pool size, a small number of investors, an LLP or partnership deed per season, and a counsel-reviewed structure in place before a single rupee moves. If that means fewer investors per season, fine.

Land

State-aware terms, short by design

In several states a tenant who farms the same land continuously can build up occupancy or purchase rights over it. That is exactly why landowners here avoid writing anything down. So the agreements are short fixed-term licenses to cultivate, drafted against the tenancy law of the state the land sits in, with renewal as a decision rather than a default — written protection for the farmer that doesn’t quietly cost the owner the land.

Proof

Proven on our own land first

Our 15 acres is the pilot. Our capital in the investor seat, our family in the grower seat, one parcel leased out on the license we drafted. We want a full season of real numbers — including the parts that went badly — before we ask an outsider to fund a crop or hand over a field.
